Can the optimization problem itself serve as the policy?

Rather than combining separately designed model-based and learning components, we formulate the optimization problem itself as the policy representation, with its defining parameters learned end-to-end from downstream objectives. The same representation accommodates model-based control, where analytical structure is retained and tuned over distributions of models and disturbances, and black-box control, where policies are learned directly from input–output behavior.

Abstract

We present an end-to-end QP-based policy framework that enables systematic policy construction with minimal domain-specific design, while preserving the transparency and interpretability of model-based control. The proposed policy representation supports both domain-randomized model-based auto-tuning, where policy parameters are optimized over distributions of disturbances and model variations, and black-box policy construction, where the problem is formulated in terms of a (possibly) unknown model without requiring explicit notions of states, inputs, or the underlying system dynamics.

We establish connections to existing policy representations and control paradigms, including robust control, multilayer perceptrons, and robot learning, and interpret our end-to-end QP policies as a common abstraction of these approaches. We validate the resulting framework in both simulation and hardware, demonstrating a broad range of applications spanning robustness, automatic policy tuning, and control under unknown system dynamics.

End-to-end QP policies

Connections to:neural network policiesrobust controlrobot learning

The control action is obtained from the solution of a parameterized quadratic program, and the parameters θ define its objective, constraints, and how the action is read out from the solution. Rather than fixing a single QP structure, the formulation can vary with the problem, from a simple projection QP to a trajectory-optimization QP with predictive dynamics, horizon coupling, costs, and constraints. Different structures induce different classes of control laws.

The parameters are optimized directly from closed-loop task performance over a batch of rollouts. Sampling initial conditions trains over a distribution of starting states, sampling plant parameters gives domain randomization over model uncertainty, and sampling disturbances exposes the policy to exogenous inputs. Because both the QP and the plant rollout are differentiable, gradients of the outer objective propagate through every closed-loop trajectory back to θ.

The key design principle is a separation between QP structure and outer objective. The QP specifies which policies can be represented, while the outer objective (an empirical mean, a risk-sensitive measure, or a worst case) determines which policy is learned, without changing the QP. This separation is what connects the framework to neural-network policies, robust control, and domain-randomized robot learning.

Connection to neural networks

The ReLU activation is exactly the Euclidean projection onto the nonnegative orthant. A single-hidden-layer ReLU policy can therefore be written as a projection QP: the QP solution gives the hidden-layer values, and the output layer maps them to the action, exactly as in the network. The ReLU network is a special case of the QP policy, and the two have the same expressive capacity: the same piecewise-affine policy class, in a different representation.

Nonlinear cart‑pole swing‑up

A linear-MPC QP relies on a local linear model and is best suited to regulation near its linearization point. The projection QP needs no predictive model. We learn its weights and biases from batches of 128 differentiable cart-pole rollouts from sampled initial conditions.

The nonnegativity constraints become active in different regions of the state space, producing different affine feedback laws. The resulting piecewise-affine policy pumps energy into the pendulum and then stabilizes it upright.

Connection to robust control

In the unconstrained linear time-invariant case, a trajectory-optimization QP reduces to linear state feedback, so learning the QP parameters selects a feedback gain. Rollout losses are aggregated over exogenous disturbances and over plant instances by two independently chosen operators. With the QP policy itself unchanged, the choice of these operators recovers familiar robust-control objectives on a double-integrator regulation task.

Lunar lander with unmodeled fuel slosh

A reusable lander must touch down carrying enough propellant for ascent and return to orbit (a propellant mass fraction of about 0.5), which produces significant slosh. The slosh is unobservable, so the MPC's internal model covers only the rigid body (vertical position, horizontal position, orientation), with thrust magnitude and gimbal angle as inputs. The training plant also includes the first slosh mode.

Two controllers share the same architecture, horizon, and constraints and differ only in how their cost weights are trained. One is trained on a fixed slosh model (no DR). The other is trained over a distribution of slosh mass, pendulum length, and damping ratio (DR). On the same fleet of 1,000 vehicles with dispersed initial conditions and slosh parameters, the DR controller's worst landing error is less than a third of the nominal controller's. Randomization costs nothing at run time: the deployed controller has the same structure as the nominal one.

Hardware experiment

We evaluate the full pipeline, state estimation and control together, on a Crazyflie quadrotor in a motion-capture arena. An industrial fan with a flow-conditioning rack produces a 5 m/s crosswind. The drone starts about 0.5 m from the fan and flies to a target 1.9 m downstream, where it must hold position.

To handle the persistent wind, the state is augmented with an estimated disturbance using a Luenberger observer. The estimated state and disturbance feed a trajectory-optimization QP, whose output is tracked by the onboard low-level controller. The QP cost parameters and the observer gains are learned jointly through differentiable closed-loop rollouts with wind of randomized direction and magnitude.

We compare against two MPC baselines hand-tuned with Bryson's rule for allowable position deviations of 10 cm and 5 cm, each with a fixed observer gain. More aggressive manual tuning led to unreliable flight. Over five trials, the learned QP policy converges substantially faster and drives the steady-state position error to zero. Both baselines converge more slowly and retain a residual error.
